About the method

The map shows how each neighbourhood voted, using the smallest unit Israel's Central Bureau of Statistics publishes: the statistical area. It follows the layout and colour rule of Sergei Kondrashov's map of the 2022 election (the "original" below). Figures on this page are for the 2026 election.

The 2026 results are provisional. They come from the Central Elections Committee's count file, not from the final results, which are due to be published in Reshumot (the official gazette) by 4 November 2026. Figures may still change slightly.

The colour rule: more than two thirds

An area takes a colour only when one bloc won more than two thirds of valid votes there. The colour is that bloc's leading party in the area. If no bloc passes two thirds, the area is pale yellow ("no bloc above 2/3").

This is a strict test on purpose. A pale yellow area is not a close race; it is any area where the losing blocs together won at least a third of the vote.

Lists that fell below the electoral threshold won no seats, but the bigger ones are shown by name, with their own colour and in their camp, so an area's leader and bloc shares are the real ones; the legend and the details panel mark them "below threshold". The smallest lists are pooled as "Other lists": they count in the total but in no bloc, so they make it slightly harder for any bloc to reach two thirds.

Stripes. Inside a coloured area, the second party of the same bloc is shown as stripes over the leader's colour when it won at least half as many votes as the leader. At most two parties are shown per area. A strong second party from a different bloc is never shown as stripes; it already shows in the area being coloured at all, or not.

Bloc view. The "Bloc" switch shows only the two camps: dark where a bloc won more than two thirds of valid votes, light where it won a majority, green where the Arab parties hold a majority (they belong to neither camp), and pale yellow where no one has a majority.

Uncommitted parties. A party that would not commit to either camp before the vote is listed as uncommitted. In the bloc view it counts for neither camp. In the leading-party view uncommitted parties are grouped together like a bloc, so an area where they win more than two thirds takes their leading party's colour.

Earlier elections. The camps changed between elections, so each election uses the camps Israeli media used at the time. Before 2019 the press spoke of a right-wing bloc and a centre-left bloc; from 2019 the dividing line was Netanyahu himself (the Anti-Netanyahu bloc); the Change bloc took its name in 2021. Parties that switched sides (Yisrael Beytenu after April 2019, Yamina and New Hope in 2021) are counted where they stood at that election. So a colour change between two elections can come from a party changing camp, not only from voters changing their vote.

The unit: statistical areas

The Central Bureau of Statistics (CBS) splits towns of roughly 10,000 people or more into statistical areas of a few thousand residents each. Smaller towns, villages, kibbutzim and moshavim are one area each. Every election is shown on the CBS 2022 boundaries, so elections can be compared: 3,857 areas, of which 2,577 have at least one polling station of their own placed in them.

Votes are counted where they are cast. An area's result is the total of the polling stations placed inside it. Voters are assigned to a station near their address, so this is close to how the area's residents voted, but not exactly: a station can serve streets on both sides of an area boundary. An area's result describes how a place voted as a whole; it does not say how any particular group of its residents voted.

Placing polling stations

The results file lists every polling station by town and station number, without coordinates. Each station was placed in a statistical area by the first method that worked, best first:

  1. One-area towns. If the town is a single statistical area, the station belongs to it. This is exact and covers most of the country's localities.
  2. Address or venue. The station's address and venue from the Central Elections Committee's official station list for that election, located with our own OpenStreetMap lookup: the house number where it is mapped, else the venue by name (usually a school), else the street. Where the address and the venue are both found but more than 500 m apart, neither is trusted. 2,761 stations were found by their street name only, with no house number or venue; they are placed at the street and may belong in a neighbouring area. The details panel says so.
  3. Nearest-numbered neighbour. Stations are numbered roughly by neighbourhood, so a station with no usable address takes the area of the nearest-numbered placed station in the same town. 688 stations were placed this way; the details panel says so.

2026. Step 2 uses the committee's official 2026 station list ("Polling Station.xlsx" on gov.il, 25 Oct 2026). Where its address and venue are not found in OpenStreetMap, the same venue's location in the 2022 list is used.

Turnout in 2026 (78%) was the highest since 1999, and Arab turnout rose from about 53% to about 80%; the count of eligible voters is defined as in earlier elections and matches the Central Elections Committee's 7,331,981. Town figures leave out double-envelope votes (soldiers, diplomats and others), which have no eligible voters of their own, so national turnout is slightly higher than the sum of the towns.

A point only counts if it falls inside its own town's boundary. That check catches most bad coordinates.

Placement is much less complete in Arab, Druze and Bedouin localities than elsewhere: there the official list often gives only the town name as the address, and the venues are often not in OpenStreetMap. Where no station in a town can be placed, every populated area of the town shows the whole town's result. Where only some can, the areas show the placed stations' result. Either way, a town's own total (search for it, or click it) is the official result for the whole town, and says how many of its votes could not be placed in an area.

Some places that vote have no CBS boundary at all, such as several Negev Bedouin tribes and, in some elections, army bases. They are outlined round their own buildings from Microsoft's building footprints or taken from OpenStreetMap, or shown as a small marker where no buildings are found. They hold 24,787 votes. CBS also publishes about 130 localities, mostly West Bank settlements, as a tiny placeholder instead of a boundary; they are drawn with the built-up area mapped in OpenStreetMap at that point (only the piece there and pieces within 200 m), or else round their own buildings, or as a small marker.

Areas without a polling station

Polling stations sit in a few buildings, mostly schools, so many neighbourhoods have residents but no station inside their own boundary; their residents vote in the school next door. Such an area shows the result of the nearest area with a station in the same town, in the same colour as everywhere else, and its details say it is an estimate. In 2026 that is 906 of the 3,483 areas with voters (26%). The estimate is only as good as the guess that residents vote at the nearest station.

An area counts as lived in when the 2022 census gives it residents, or when OpenStreetMap maps at least 15% of it (and at least 3 hectares) as housing; the second test catches neighbourhoods the census file leaves out. CBS publishes one population figure for some groups of neighbouring areas; the details panel shows that shared figure and says how many areas share it.

In East Jerusalem, large neighbourhoods have no polling station because most residents are not citizens and cannot vote for the Knesset. There, an area whose whole census group has no station takes the result of the nearest area in the city where Arab parties won, not of the nearest station, which is often in a Jewish neighbourhood. This choice depends on the result itself; it follows the original map.

Drawing

Areas nobody lives in stay pale cream, as in the original map. Built-up land where nobody lives, such as ports, refineries, industrial zones and business parks, is grey: areas of at most 10 km² at least half covered by industrial, commercial or campus land in OpenStreetMap.

Some big statistical areas are mostly empty land. In towns with several statistical areas, areas of 5 km² or more that are less than a quarter built up are drawn only where people live (Dimona, Arad, Eilat and other city outskirts); so are single-area localities of 50 km² or more that are less than 15% built up (Mitzpe Ramon). "Where people live" is land within reach of buildings in Microsoft's building footprints, keeping the main settlement and sizeable patches within 1 km of it. Villages, kibbutzim, moshavim and Bedouin localities keep their CBS shapes.

Areas stop at the coastline and at lakes, harbours and marinas, all taken from OpenStreetMap; the Sea of Galilee and the Dead Sea show as water. In towns with several statistical areas, parks of 30 hectares or more are cut out of coloured areas. On blank land, OpenStreetMap parks show green, beaches sand, and industry, ports and civil airports grey; the biggest are named when you zoom in close.

None of this changes any area's votes or figures.

Double envelopes and other votes off the map

Soldiers, hospital patients, prisoners, diplomats and some others vote in "double envelopes". These are counted together in one national pseudo-locality with no address, so they cannot be mapped. In 2026 they came to 449,357 valid votes, 7.9% of the national total of 5,690,682. The map's side panel has a box giving them by bloc and by party, so they are not lost. They are in no area's or town's result. Because these voters are not spread evenly (most are soldiers), places with many soldiers are slightly under-represented in their local results.

A further 205,566 votes come from stations that could not be placed in any statistical area. They are counted in their town's total but not drawn.

What is approximate

  • Stations placed from the street name only, or by a nearest-numbered neighbour (both said in the details panel).
  • Areas with residents but no station of their own, which show the nearest station's area (said in the details panel).
  • Towns where few or no stations could be placed, mostly Arab, Druze and Bedouin localities.
  • Which land is lived in or built up: the census where it has a figure, OpenStreetMap land use otherwise. The OpenStreetMap data is current, so a neighbourhood built after the election can count as lived in.
  • Outlines of settlements CBS gives only as a placeholder, of localities without a CBS boundary, and of big, thinly settled areas.
  • Area boundaries are simplified for the web: to about 3 metres when zoomed in, and more coarsely when zoomed out, where the difference is smaller than a screen pixel.
  • Towns founded after the 2022 CBS boundaries were drawn are shown as the combined boundary of the localities they replaced.
  • Small areas with few voters can swing a lot on a handful of votes; the details panel flags areas with fewer than 100 valid votes.

Differences from the original

The layout and the two-thirds rule follow Sergei Kondrashov's map of the 25th Knesset results. Two things differ. This map groups parties into the two camps Israeli media used at each election (in 2022 and 2026 the Bibi bloc, Netanyahu's parties, and the Change bloc, the parties of the Bennett-Lapid government), plus the Arab parties. The original used ideological trends instead: it counts Yisrael Beytenu with the right and National Unity with neither camp, which is why Ariel and parts of the Tel Aviv coast look different. This map also adds the double-envelope box. Nothing from the original image or its data is reused; every number here was rebuilt from public sources.

Using the map

  • Hover over an area, or tap it on a phone, to see its results. The panel gives bloc shares, every party above half a percent, and any caveats. A click or tap keeps that place in the panel until you close it or click empty land.
  • Type a town in English or Hebrew in the search box to zoom to it and see the town's official total.
  • In the One party view, "Top places" lists the 25 places where the party did best (places with at least 1,000 valid votes).
  • The Change view colours each area by how much a bloc's or a party's share of valid votes rose or fell since an earlier election you choose, in percentage points. The statistical areas are the same 2022 areas in every election, so an area is compared with itself. Areas without a polling station of their own show the result they borrow in each election (see above); grey means one of the two elections has no result there. A party is offered only if it ran in both elections. This is a comparison of areas, not of the same voters, and populations change.
  • Second map beside it shows another election next to this one; the two maps move together, and the panel gives both elections for the place under the pointer. On a phone the maps are one above the other.
  • Bloc coding lets you move any list to another bloc (or to neither): every bloc colour, figure and the Change view follow. Lists such as Yamina (2021), Ra'am and Yisrael Beytenu are contested, and the choice moves results: with Yamina counted in neither bloc, the right bloc's average rise in an area from 2021 to 2022 is about 7.5 points, against about 1.6 with Yamina in the right bloc. A changed coding is kept in the address.
  • Keyboard: / jumps to search; arrow keys pick a town and Enter zooms to it. Click the map (or tab to it) and use the arrow keys to move, + and - to zoom, and Enter to read the area under the cross in the middle.
  • The address bar keeps the election, the view, the party, the selected place, the second map, any change to the bloc coding and the map position, so a link (or Copy link) opens exactly what you see.
